## How EMI is calculated
An Equated Monthly Instalment (EMI) uses the reducing-balance method: EMI = P x r x (1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n - 1), where P is the principal, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate / 12 / 100) and n is the number of monthly instalments. In the early years most of each EMI is interest; the principal share grows over time. A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises the total interest paid.

## Obligation-to-income ratio (FOIR)
Lenders commonly check that total monthly loan obligations, including the new EMI, stay within roughly 40 to 50 percent of net monthly income. The exact limit varies by lender, income level and credit score. A lower ratio improves approval chances and leaves room for savings.

## Fixed versus floating interest rates
A floating-rate loan changes when the lender's benchmark changes. For new retail floating-rate loans, banks link rates to an external benchmark such as the RBI repo rate. Fixed-rate loans keep the same rate for a set period. When floating rates fall, existing borrowers may need to ask for a reset or switch to benefit.

## Prepayment and foreclosure
RBI rules bar banks and most regulated lenders from charging foreclosure or pre-payment penalties on floating-rate loans taken by individual borrowers for non-business purposes. Fixed-rate loans can carry prepayment charges, so read the sanction letter. Part-prepaying early in the tenure saves the most interest.

## Credit score and loan pricing
Credit bureaus such as CIBIL give a score from 300 to 900. Scores of about 750 and above are generally treated as good and often earn better interest rates. Late EMI payments, high credit-card utilisation and many loan enquiries in a short time lower the score.

## Other loan costs to check
Besides interest, check the processing fee, documentation charges, insurance bundled with the loan, and any penal charges for late payment. Compare lenders using the total cost of borrowing, not only the headline interest rate.
